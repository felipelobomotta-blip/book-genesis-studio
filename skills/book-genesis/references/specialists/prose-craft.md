# Prose craft specialist

Line-level craft for openings, chapter endings and dialogue, plus the revision moves against predictability that only Phase 5: Revision Loop applies.

When to load: Phase 2: Architecture (orchestrator) for artifacts/08-opening-strategy.md; Phase 3: Drafting (writer), as craft knowledge; Phase 4: Adversarial Audit (auditor), as a diagnostic lens and to name revision moves in tickets; Phase 5: Revision Loop (orchestrator writing tickets, revision editor applying them), the only phase in which the revision moves in section 6 apply. Never give this file to the blind reader.

## Scope

- **Rhythm** belongs to the Rhythm contract, defined in references/specialists/narrative-foundation.md and filled in for each book in artifacts/05-voice.md. This file sets no rhythm; wherever it says "read aloud" or "vary", check against that contract.
- **Numeric prose ranges** (dialogue share, adverb density, tag use, readability) live in references/patterns/prose.md. Comp measurements in artifacts/01-market-map.md override them for this book. Numbers marked "house default" here are Book Genesis conventions, not market findings, and comp measurements override them too.
- **AI-tell detection** lives in references/patterns/ai-tells-en.md and references/patterns/ai-tells-pt-br.md and runs in Phase 4: Adversarial Audit and Phase 5: Revision Loop, not while drafting.
- **While drafting**, sections 1-5 are craft knowledge. Every checklist runs after the text exists, never mid-sentence.

## 1. Openings

The first pages carry more weight than any other part of the book: they are what an agent, an editor and a buyer reading the free sample see first. For the opening data (verbs over adjectives, where the inciting incident lands) see references/patterns/prose.md.

### Chapter-one strategy (artifacts/08-opening-strategy.md)

Choose on purpose. Never fall back on "a competent professional meets an anomaly", the default opening of model-written drafts.

| Strategy | What it does | Comps |
|---|---|---|
| Voice bomb | hooks by how the story is told | The Catcher in the Rye (Salinger); Lolita (Nabokov) |
| In medias res | drops the reader mid-scene with no setup; the disorientation is the hook | The Road (McCarthy); Beloved (Morrison) |
| Wrong emotion | humor in a tragedy, gravity in a comedy, calm in a thriller | Vonnegut; Ishiguro |
| Confession | the narrator admits what they should not | Lolita (Nabokov); Gone Girl (Flynn) |
| The question | opens a question the reader cannot drop | Rebecca (du Maurier); Nineteen Eighty-Four (Orwell) |
| Ordinary made strange | a mundane scene that feels wrong before the reader knows why | "The Lottery" (Shirley Jackson); Never Let Me Go (Ishiguro) |
| The failure | meets the character in weakness, confusion or error | Project Hail Mary (Weir) |

Record the strategy and why it fits this book (genre-aware, not genre-predictable), three to five first-line options, the promise the first page makes, and how chapter 1 ends.

### Five layers of hook

1. **First line.** One job: stop the reader from stopping. Four types that work:
   - *Immediate conflict:* situation and consequence in one sentence. "I smoked a joint on a beach called Serenity and never had any serenity again."
   - *Irresistible voice:* a tone so specific the reader follows the voice before the plot. "My mother called at six in the morning to tell me I looked sad on Instagram."
   - *Withheld information:* a promise of revelation. "Three things nobody tells you about electroconvulsive therapy."
   - *Unforgettable image:* vivid and unexpected enough to stick. "The psychiatrist's office had a painting of sunflowers on the wall. The session cost four hundred an hour. The sunflowers were free."
2. **First paragraph.** Sets voice and tone: in three or four sentences the reader knows what kind of book this is. Write it at the Rhythm contract's baseline, so the reader learns the book's default before any pressure moves it. A long lyrical paragraph promises a contemplative book; short dry paragraphs, a direct one; a mix of registers, a book that surprises.
3. **First page.** Creates commitment. By its end the reader has a question they want answered, a character they want to know more about, and a sense of the tone.
4. **First scene.** Delivers the genre promise. A mental-health memoir makes the reader feel the vulnerability; a thriller, the danger; a comic novel, a laugh.
5. **First chapter.** Makes the book hard to put down. By its end the reader knows what is at stake, cares about at least one character, and has a concrete reason to open chapter 2.

### Opening tests (after the draft exists)

1. **Removal:** delete the first sentence. If nothing is lost, rewrite it.
2. **"So what?":** ask it of every sentence on page one. If the answer is "nothing", the sentence is not working.
3. **Read aloud:** a stumble marks a problem. Check the passage against the Rhythm contract before changing it.
4. **Genre:** could someone who reads only page one say what kind of book this is?

## 2. Chapter endings that pull

- Every chapter carries a value shift: the protagonist's situation turns from positive to negative or the reverse (Coyne, *The Story Grid*). No shift, dead chapter.
- The last page loads a question sharper than the one the chapter resolved. Resolve one, open two.
- Not every ending is a cliffhanger. An unanswered question, a shifted emotion or an image that lingers all pull. Alternate hard hooks with quieter endings; references/patterns/index.md points to the chapter-ending and reader-fatigue findings.
- The last line is specific, never a caption. "But maybe it was too late." becomes "The form expired three months ago. He never opened the envelope."

After drafting, check: does the ending type differ from the previous chapter's? Does the end emotion match artifacts/06-emotional-curve.md, or depart from it on purpose?

## 3. Dialogue

### Four functions

Every exchange serves at least one. An exchange that serves none is cut.

1. **Reveal character.** Word choice shows class, education and personality; what someone chooses not to say shows as much as what they say. Speech cadence comes from the character's voice card in artifacts/05-voice.md.
2. **Advance conflict.** Every exchange changes the state of the scene. Calm at the start and calm at the end: cut or rewrite the scene.
3. **Transmit information.** The most dangerous function. Test: is a character explaining something to the reader rather than to the other character?
   - Bad: "As you know, we met ten years ago when we worked together at the factory."
   - Good: "Ten years and you still haven't learned to close the door."
4. **Create tension.** What goes unsaid matters more. Two people discussing the weather while one knows the other is lying is tension; two people arguing openly is drama without subtext.

### Subtext

Characters almost never say what they think. The distance between what they say and what they feel is the subtext.

- Without: "I'm angry because you abandoned me."
- With: "What time do you get in tomorrow?" (She does not want the time. She wants to know whether he will show up this time.)

Techniques: the oblique answer (answering something other than what was asked); the contradicting action ("I'm fine" while the glass breaks in her hand); the change of subject (the dodge is the answer); silence.

Planning check for every significant exchange, in the outline or in revision: write one line, *surface topic / real topic*. If the two are the same, the dialogue is flat.

### Real speech is messy

People interrupt, mid-word if needed. They repeat themselves under stress, answer the wrong thing because they were thinking of something else, trail off, and let a silence run for a paragraph. Orderly turn-taking reads as machine dialogue.

### Distinct voices

Two characters never sound alike. Separate them on vocabulary (formal and technical, or colloquial and slangy), sentence length (clipped or explanatory), verbal tic, certainty (assertive or hesitant) and humor (sarcastic or literal). Write from the voice cards in artifacts/05-voice.md and test with the cover-the-name test in references/specialists/narrative-foundation.md.

### Tags and beats

"Said" and "asked" are the invisible default (Elmore Leonard's rule; Blatt's counts are in references/patterns/prose.md). Save action beats for the moments that matter.

- Before: "I don't know," he said nervously.
- After: "I don't know." He squeezed the pen cap until the plastic cracked.

"Nervously" tells; the cracking plastic shows. Never use showy speech verbs ("vociferated", "exclaimed", "murmured thoughtfully"): they pull attention to themselves and out of the scene.

### Dialogue-to-narration balance

Take the range from references/patterns/prose.md, and the comps' measured share from artifacts/01-market-map.md where it exists; for this book the comps win. After drafting, measure each chapter's dialogue share, counting dialogue by the language's own convention: quotation marks in English; in Brazilian Portuguese, usually lines that open with a dash (the travessão). Outside the range, fix it at the scene level: turn summarized conversation into dialogue, or the reverse.

### Reported speech (memoir and non-fiction)

Real people speak, and reported speech must sound alive without inventing anything.

- Flat: "The psychiatrist said I should consider medication."
- Alive: "The psychiatrist took off his glasses, looked at me the way you look at a car with a blown engine, and said, 'We're going to need medication.'"

Techniques: an action beat before the line; partial speech ("He said something about resistance. I wasn't listening."); indirect speech that keeps the voice ("My mother had a theory about depression. It involved cold showers and God.").

Honesty rule: if you do not remember the exact words, no quotation marks. Use indirect speech. Memoir is not journalism, and it is not fiction either.

## 4. Non-fiction prose

### Data as narrative

Raw data is cold; data in context lands. The placeholders below stand for figures from the chapter's evidence package; never invent one.

- Report: "According to a [year] survey by [agency], [N]% of people aged 18 to 29 have never held a formal work contract."
- Narrative with data: "[N]% of people my age have never signed a work contract. That is [agency]'s count. What [agency] does not count is how many of them were called lazy at the family dinner table."

Moves:

- **Data to personal consequence:** the number, plus what it means in the reader's life.
- **Data as confirmation:** the reader already feels it and the number validates it. "You are not the only one. There are [N] million of us."
- **Data as surprise:** the number contradicts what the reader assumes.
- **Data as weapon:** in social critique, dry and without comment; the number speaks.

Rules: two or three data points per page at most (house default); alternate data, reflection and narrative, and never put three data points in a row; attribute in the sentence ("[agency] shows that...") or in an endnote, never with an academic footnote apparatus. Every figure comes from an evidence package (references/specialists/research.md).

### Argument that flows

- Dissertation: "Three factors drive the precarity of young people's work. The first is informality. The second is gig platforms. The third is the devaluation of degrees."
- Narrative with a thesis: "My first job was off the books. The second was an app. The third required a master's degree and paid less than the app. If that isn't precarity, somebody explain it to me."

Let the thesis grow out of lived experience; progress through concrete scenes instead of "first, second, third"; let the reader deduce the thesis and feel it is theirs. A thesis that has to be stated means the text has not done its work.

## 5. Line quality: what an editor underlines

Underlined in approval:

- **Specificity:** "seventy-eight thousand" over "a lot of money"; a named beach over "a beach".
- **Concrete image over essay:** "The screen fills the gap the script left" over "Social media compensates for a lack of purpose."
- **A strong verb instead of verb plus adverb:** "ran quickly" becomes "bolted"; "shut the door hard" becomes "slammed the door".
- **The sentence a reader rereads** by choice, out of admiration rather than confusion.

Underlined in red:

- **Cliché:** "light at the end of the tunnel", "a double-edged sword", "the last straw". If you have heard it, so has the reader.
- **Fat:** "basically", "literally" (when it is not literal), "really", "very". Cut.
- **Abstraction:** "felt a deep existential anguish". What did the body feel? A tight chest, shaking hands, nausea? Show that.
- **Passive without a reason:** "The diagnosis was given by the doctor" becomes "The doctor gave the diagnosis."
- **Adjective stacks:** "the sad, grey, melancholy morning" becomes "Grey morning." One strong adjective beats three weak ones.

Chapter check, after drafting and in Phase 4: Adversarial Audit:

1. Read aloud. Where you stumble, check the passage against the Rhythm contract.
2. Find three sentences an editor would underline in approval. None means the prose is functional, not distinct.
3. Find three sentences carrying fat, and cut them.
4. At least one concrete image per page. Fewer means the chapter has gone abstract.
5. Paragraph lengths vary within the Rhythm contract's local limits.

## 6. Revision moves against predictability (Phase 5: Revision Loop only)

These are Phase 5: Revision Loop tools and are never applied while drafting. In Phase 3: Drafting the writer writes; these moves work on text that already exists, and only through tickets. The revision editor makes no edit without a ticket and changes only the ticket's span (references/specialists/revision-editor.md), so each move reaches the page as a ticket that names it.

A competent chapter can be well built, consistent in voice, coherent in theme and still predictable. The moves break exactly the right things so the chapter can breathe. They are not a rewrite.

### Writing move tickets (orchestrator, auditor)

- **Evidence first.** Ticket a move when the panel records a two-vote defect flagged `predictable`, `machine_prose`, `over_explained` or `stiff_dialogue`, or when an audit pass (over-explanation, human mess, failure, machine prose) quotes the passage. A hunch is not a ticket.
- **Class and span.** A move is a prose-class ticket whose span is the quoted passage; the missing-paragraph move spans the one paragraph it would cut. Name the move in the ticket's `smallest fix` line.
- **Dose by quality, not quota.** Strong chapter (already alive, varied, noisy): no more than 2-4 move tickets; do not force noise into text that is already alive. Competent but predictable: 5-6; this is the main target. Weak (flat, generic, pattern-heavy): 6-8. Never more than 8 in one chapter: past that the problem is structural, so write a structural ticket instead.
- **Carry what a prose packet lacks.** On a prose ticket the revision editor receives the chapter and artifacts/05-voice.md, not the character or outline files. An irrelevant-thought ticket therefore quotes the character's noise profile from artifacts/03-characters.md, and every move ticket's "Preserve" line names the chapter's anchor (artifacts/07-outline.md) and any re-read reward in or near the span (artifacts/06-emotional-curve.md).
- **Leave deliberate deviations alone.** Do not ticket a passage the writer marked in work/ as a deliberate deviation unless readers stumble on it: it is often the most human part.

### The eight moves

Each move says what a ticket targets and how the revision editor repairs it inside the span.

1. **Simile surgery** (highest priority). Target: a figure that explains itself, an image followed by an extension that unpacks it, a frequent tell in model-written drafts (references/patterns/ai-tells-en.md). Repair: if the extension adds meaning the reader cannot infer, keep it; if it restates, cut the extension and leave the image raw.
   - Before: "Rosa Marsh has become a student of this investigation the way some people become students of the disease that's killing them: total immersion, every detail cataloged, because the alternative is sitting still and waiting."
   - After: "Rosa Marsh has become a student of this investigation the way some people become students of the disease that's killing them."

   The ceiling is the one in the AI-tell reference for the book's language (references/patterns/ai-tells-en.md or references/patterns/ai-tells-pt-br.md), plus any exception recorded in artifacts/05-voice.md. Cut to the ceiling, not to zero: a chapter stripped of every figure loses its voice.
2. **Irrelevant thought.** Target: a character locked on the plot for pages, in a chapter below the noise dose set in references/specialists/narrative-foundation.md (noise profile); a chapter already at the dose gets no ticket. Repair: insert one thought that has nothing to do with the scene, taken from the noise profile the ticket quotes. No justification, no reflection on why it came; one or two sentences; then the scene continues as if it had not happened.

   > She stared at the board. Fourteen cases. Fourteen women. The corkboard was running out of space.
   > She wondered if the Thai place on Burnside had changed its menu again. They'd taken off the papaya salad last month and she was still angry about it.
   > The pushpin for Elena Marsh was yellow. She didn't remember choosing yellow.
3. **Emotional control break.** Target: a character who notices a feeling, manages it and moves on, every time. Repair: make the management fail, once. Small, not melodramatic: the voice cracks, the eyes burn, the hand keeps shaking after it is told to stop. The prose does not explain the break, the character does not reflect on it, and other characters may or may not notice.
4. **Precision deflation.** Target: three to five falsely precise numbers that exist only to look concrete ("She'd worked 247 cases in the last decade"; "The signal arrived at 14:23:07 UTC"). Repair: replace some with human vagueness ("more cases than she could count"; "in the early afternoon, while half the team was at lunch"). Keep precision that serves the story: a detective counting evidence, a scientist reading instruments, world-building figures the reader tracks.
5. **Rough sentence.** Target: a chapter without the rough sentence the Rhythm contract requires; the ticket's span is a quiet passage. Repair: add one there, following the contract's definition; it stays inside the envelope. If you catch yourself making it clever, it is not rough.
6. **Negation break.** Target: the "Not X. Not Y. [Z]." opener, repeated. Repair: rewrite at least half of them as direct assertion. "Not a blip. Not a transient. A sustained carrier wave." becomes "A sustained carrier wave, steady as a heartbeat."
7. **Missing paragraph.** Target: the most predictable paragraph, the one you could guess before reading it: it explains what just happened, summarizes the character's emotional state, transitions too smoothly, or says what the reader already worked out. Repair: run the revision editor's burden-of-proof test for deletion; if the paragraph has no other function, cut it. If something essential would go with it, rewrite it to be unpredictable instead.
8. **Dialogue mess.** Target: the cleanest, most orderly exchange in the chapter. Repair: add one or two of the behaviors in "Real speech is messy" (section 3).

### Protection checks

For literary fiction, speculative fiction, and any book whose comps reward rereading:

- Do not deflate numbers that carry world-building, or dates the attentive reader tracks.
- Never ticket the missing-paragraph move on a paragraph that holds a re-read reward (check artifacts/07-outline.md and artifacts/06-emotional-curve.md first).
- Go lighter on simile surgery where dense figurative prose is part of the comps' appeal, citing the comp passage recorded in artifacts/05-voice.md.

### Boundaries

- Modify existing prose inside the ticket's span only: no new scenes, plot points or characters.
- Keep the voice. Every change must sound like this book, not like another author.
- Keep the chapter's anchor, the image, moment or line recorded for it in artifacts/06-emotional-curve.md. A move sharpens it, never dissolves it.
- Keep cadence inside the Rhythm contract's envelope.
- Record each move as its ticket's line in the revision report defined in references/specialists/revision-editor.md, naming the move.
