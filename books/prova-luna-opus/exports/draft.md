# Chapter 1: The Call Light

At 2:13 a.m., Mrs. Halloway decides the ceiling is lying to her.

“It says there’s a man up there,” she tells me.

I look at the ceiling. Acoustic tiles. Water stain above the sprinkler head. One fluorescent panel with a dead moth trapped inside it.

“The ceiling is empty,” I say.

“He moved when you looked.”

“That’s generally how men avoid being useful.”

She studies me, deciding whether this is humor or a symptom. At eighty-six, she has learned not to waste either. Her oxygen tubing loops beneath her nostrils, the skin around them pink and rubbed. Saturation ninety-four percent on two liters. Heart rate eighty-eight. Respirations twenty, shallow but even.

“What did he look like?”

“Wet.”

Outside, the sea throws itself at the windows.

Mrs. Halloway’s room is the first on the east side of Ward C, which means she gets the full performance: rain striking the glass, wind pushing through the frames, pipes knocking somewhere in the wall. St. Brigid’s was built before anyone thought hospitals should be quiet. The building settles, complains, and occasionally sheds pieces of itself. Last month, a ceiling tile fell into the medication room. Nobody had been standing beneath it, so the incident was classified as contained.

I check her pupils, listen to her lungs, press two fingers to the radial pulse she insists is “too fast.”

“Do you know where you are?”

“St. Brigid’s.”

“What month?”

“November.”

“It’s October.”

“Then I’m ahead.”

“By several weeks.”

She gives me a narrow smile. Her hands are cold. I pull the blanket up to her shoulders and make a note on the observation sheet. At the bottom of the page, under *behavioral presentation*, I write: *Reports visual hallucination of wet male figure on ceiling. Alert and oriented. No acute respiratory change.*

The words are small and orderly. They behave better than people.

Mrs. Halloway catches my wrist before I can turn away.

“Don’t let them move me,” she says.

“Who?”

“The men.”

“It’s just us in here.”

“Not yet.”

Her fingers tighten once, then loosen. I feel the pulse in them. Eighty-eight, maybe ninety. The body keeps its own records.

“I’ll be back in ten minutes,” I say.

“You always say that.”

“I’m very consistent.”

“You’re not.”

The call light above her bed blinks red.

I press the cancel button.

The corridor is empty except for the medication cart, two linen bags, and Nia Okafor standing at the nurses’ station with her phone held against one ear. She is younger than me by enough years to still believe sleep can be recovered later. Her left thumbnail is painted green, the polish chipped down the middle.

“No, I know what the policy says,” she says into the phone. “I’m asking what you want me to do with the actual patient.”

She sees me and points toward the medication room.

I mouth, *Who is it?*

She mouths back, *Pharmacy.*

Then she turns away, shoulder raised, listening.

The ward has eighteen beds. Tonight we have twenty-one patients, one bed in the day room, and an empty isolation room occupied by a man waiting for transport because the transport company has no driver willing to cross the causeway in this weather. The electronic board displays the names in green and amber. Green means stable. Amber means someone has offended the machine by requiring attention.

Three amber names belong to me.

The first is Mrs. Halloway.

The second is Mr. Pritchard, room six, whose blood pressure has been climbing since midnight. His antibiotic is due at one-thirty. Pharmacy sent the dose to the wrong tower. The replacement is somewhere between the pneumatic tube station and the basement, where all lost things eventually become administrative concerns.

The third is Len Torres, room twelve, who has pulled off his telemetry leads twice and is now convinced the wires are listening to him.

I open the medication room. The antibiotic bin is empty.

“Still nothing?” Nia asks, covering the phone.

“Nothing.”

“Pharmacy says it was sent.”

“Pharmacy says many things.”

“Did you check the tube station?”

“I checked it twice.”

“Check again.”

She says it lightly, but the phrase lands where it always does.

I check again.

The tube station is behind the nurses’ station, beside the supply alcove. I open the carrier. Empty except for a wad of gauze and a plastic cap from a syringe. I check the return rack, the floor, the ledge beneath the control panel. No antibiotic.

The coffee is bad and I drink all of it.

“Found a cap?” Nia asks.

“Not the medication.”

“Could be in transit.”

“It’s been nearly an hour.”

“I can call them back.”

“They know.”

“Knowing and doing aren’t twins.”

She returns to the phone. “No, I’m still here. I’m not being difficult. I’m being—” She stops, listens, and her face changes at whatever she hears. Her mouth goes flat.

“My sister’s school is closed?” she says. “No, I’m at work. I can’t just—”

She turns away again, pressing two fingers to the bridge of her nose.

I take the chart for Mr. Pritchard and walk to room six.

He is asleep on his left side, mouth open, one hand outside the blanket. His blood pressure cuff has loosened and is hanging around his arm like a collapsed belt. I retake it manually: 184 over 96.

“Mr. Pritchard.”

He opens his eyes.

“Are we done?”

“Not yet.”

“I knew there was a catch.”

I check his pulse, temperature, pupils. No chest pain. No headache. No new weakness. He says the word *fine* three times, each time less convincingly.

“The antibiotic is late,” I tell him.

“Late like a train?”

“Late like someone has misplaced the train.”

“Ah.”

“I’m going to recheck your pressure in fifteen minutes.”

“You said that before.”

“I say many things.”

“Your coffee smells like wet cardboard.”

“That’s because it’s hospital coffee.”

“It’s a Tuesday.”

“It’s Thursday.”

“Then it’s worse than I thought.”

I adjust the cuff and write down the reading. At the door, the corridor lights flicker.

Once.

Twice.

Then all six panels on the east side go dark.

The emergency strips along the floor come on, thin blue lines that make the ward look submerged. For a few seconds, nobody speaks. The monitors continue their small, obedient beeping. Somewhere behind me, a patient laughs.

The lights return.

Mrs. Halloway’s call light is on again.

I go back.

She is sitting upright, oxygen tubing pulled loose. The blanket has fallen to the floor. Her face is gray around the mouth.

“Where is he?” she asks.

“Who?”

“The wet man.”

Her respirations are twenty-eight now. I reconnect the oxygen and place the pulse oximeter on her finger. Saturation eighty-seven, climbing slowly.

“Don’t move,” I say.

“I’m not moving.”

“You are sitting up.”

“I can do two things.”

“Apparently.”

The saturation reaches ninety-one. I listen to her lungs. Fine crackles at the bases, more on the right. She has been admitted for fluid overload, and her furosemide is due at two-thirty. I check the medication administration record. The order is active. The dose is in the cart.

I draw it up and scan the barcode.

The scanner flashes red.

*Medication not found in assigned location.*

I look at the syringe in my hand.

“That’s rude,” I say.

Mrs. Halloway’s fingers close around the blanket.

The scanner rejects it again. I check the patient band. Correct name, correct date of birth. I check the medication label. Correct drug, correct dose, correct time.

“Check again,” I whisper.

I scan.

Red.

The lights flicker a third time.

Nia appears in the doorway. “What happened?”

“Scanner’s being dramatic.”

“Like the rest of us?”

“Her saturation dropped.”

Nia steps inside and looks at the monitor. “Ninety-two.”

“Was eighty-seven.”

“Her lungs?”

“Basilar crackles.”

“I’ll call the resident.”

“Call pharmacy first. I need the Pritchard antibiotic.”

“I’m already on them.”

“Call again.”

“I am calling again.”

Her voice sharpens at the end. At the wall phone, at the weather, at the school that closed without consulting her schedule. She picks up the receiver and begins punching numbers.

I sit beside Mrs. Halloway. Her skin is cool. Her breathing has steadied but remains fast enough to make the muscles beneath her ribs move.

“Look at me,” I say.

She does.

“Can you tell me your full name?”

“Eleanor Halloway.”

“Where are you?”

“St. Brigid’s.”

“What did you see?”

“A man.”

“Where?”

She lifts one hand toward the ceiling. The gesture stops halfway.

“On the other side.”

“Of the ceiling?”

“Of the room.”

“There isn’t another room above us.”

“There used to be.”

The monitor gives a single high note, then returns to its rhythm.

I look up.

The water stain has spread along the seam. A brown line curves across the tile and disappears behind the light panel. For half a second it resembles a handprint seen through dirty glass.

Dishwashers. Why did people load forks upward? The tines were supposed to point down. Everyone knew that. Everyone except—

Mrs. Halloway says, “Nurse?”

I blink.

“Yes.”

“You went away.”

“I’m here.”

She keeps watching the ceiling.

Nia hangs up. “They’re sending the resident.”

“Which resident?”

“Dr. Bell.”

“Then they’re sending the resident who is already here.”

“Exactly. Efficient.”

The pager clipped to my waistband sounds.

It is not a beep. It is a dry mechanical chirp, two notes close together, the same sound it has made every night for years. I pull it free.

The screen shows a single line of text.

`ROOM 0: NEED WATER. PLEASE CHECK BREATHING.`

I read it once.

The ward continues around me. A monitor alarms in room twelve. Someone calls for a blanket. Nia says, “I’m coming,” though nobody has asked her anything.

I read it again.

`ROOM 0: NEED WATER. PLEASE CHECK BREATHING.`

The message is a patient call: location, request, instruction. The system sends pages from beds, not rooms. A nurse enters a room number when she assigns a handset. Room 0 is not assigned to anything.

Room 0 was gone.

I have worked at St. Brigid’s for nearly a decade. The east ward begins at Room 1. Before the renovation, the numbering began at 2 because Room 1 was used for storage. Before that, the old west service wing had a room marked 0 on a maintenance plan. The wing was sealed and rebuilt around it two years ago.

I know this because I signed the transfer inventory.

The page remains on the screen.

Nia looks over. “What?”

“Nothing.”

She crosses the room. “That’s not your nothing face.”

“I got a page.”

“From?”

I turn the display toward her.

She reads it. Her eyebrows lift, then settle.

“Room zero?”

“Probably a routing error.”

“There’s no room zero.”

“I’m aware.”

“Is this one of those test messages?”

“No. Test pages say *system verification*.”

“Maybe somebody typed it in.”

“Who?”

She looks toward the darkened corridor. “Somebody with a deeply underdeveloped sense of humor.”

“Probably.”

My fingers have begun to shake. I set the pager on the bed table. It knocks against the plastic cup. I reach for it, miss, and catch it against my thigh.

It is a small thing, the refusal. My arm remains at my side. The pager is warm against my palm.

Nia glances at Mrs. Halloway. “Did you check the breathing?”

“I’m checking it now.”

“Room zero’s?”

I look at the message.

“Patient’s,” I say.

Mrs. Halloway opens her eyes. “I need water.”

The sentence is too exact.

I give her a sip from the plastic cup on the table. She coughs once, and I stop. Her breathing changes immediately—faster, wet at the edges. I sit her higher and call for suction. Nia reaches for the wall button.

“Don’t,” Mrs. Halloway says.

Her voice is suddenly clear.

“Don’t let them move me.”

“You’re staying here,” I tell her.

The door opens behind us.

Dr. Elias Venn stands in the corridor in shirtsleeves, his white coat folded over one arm. He has a pale crescent scar across his right thumb, visible when he holds a chart. He looks first at the monitor, then at the oxygen tubing, then at me. He never begins with the person.

“What happened?” he asks.

“Transient desaturation,” I say. “Eighty-seven percent, now ninety-three on two liters. Crackles at both bases. Furosemide dose is ready but the scanner is rejecting the assignment.”

“Give it.”

“I’m waiting for pharmacy to confirm the override.”

He smiles as if I have offered him a manageable inconvenience. “Mara, this is not the hour for procedural theater.”

“It’s not theater if the medication record is wrong.”

“Then document the variance and administer.”

“I’m checking the order.”

“You’ve checked it.”

“I’m checking again.”

His smile thins.

Nia steps between the medication cart and the doorway. “The resident is on the way.”

“Good,” Elias says. “And Mr. Pritchard?”

“Antibiotic delayed nearly an hour,” I say.

“Why?”

“Pharmacy sent it to the wrong tower.”

“Then the variance will be documented.”

“His pressure is 184 systolic.”

“Recheck after the dose.”

“There is no dose.”

He looks at me. His eyes are steady, the color of the sea before the weather turns.

“Find it,” he says.

The pager chirps again.

Everyone stops.

The screen has changed.

`ROOM 0: NEED WATER. PLEASE CHECK BREATHING.`

No new message. The same message, displayed again.

Elias holds out his hand. “Give me that.”

I do not.

“What exactly are you concerned about?” he asks.

“Nothing yet.”

“Then you should avoid creating concern where none exists.”

Nia’s phone vibrates on the counter. She glances at it and turns it facedown without reading the notification. Her chipped green thumbnail catches beneath the edge of the case.

Mrs. Halloway says, “The men are waiting.”

Elias looks at her. His expression does not change, but his attention shifts to the ceiling.

“Has she received anything?” he asks.

“No new medications,” I say.

“Any history of hallucinations?”

“Not documented.”

“Could be hypoxia.”

“She was alert before the desaturation.”

“Hypoxia is not obligated to follow your preferred sequence.”

He moves toward the bed. Mrs. Halloway reaches for him, but he does not take her hand. He checks her pupils with the penlight attached to his badge reel, then listens to her chest.

“Fluid overload,” he says. “Administer the diuretic.”

“I’m waiting for the override.”

“Of course you are.”

He says it gently.

That is how Elias threatens people. He makes the room around them agree that they are being difficult.

The resident arrives carrying a tablet and no pen. Dr. Bell is young enough to apologize before she has made a mistake. She examines Mrs. Halloway, reviews the chart, and places the furosemide order again.

The scanner accepts it this time.

“Interesting,” Nia says.

“Technical issue,” Elias says.

The medication goes in. Mrs. Halloway’s saturation improves to ninety-five. Her breathing slows.

Elias checks the clock.

“Storm protocol begins at three,” he says. “All nonessential movement stops after that. We’re conserving staff for the emergency department.”

“Are we expecting an evacuation?” I ask.

“Not at this stage.”

“Then why restrict movement?”

“Patient flow.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the answer we have.”

He turns to leave.

“Dr. Venn,” I say.

He pauses.

“Where does the system route a page from Room 0?”

For the first time tonight, his face becomes completely still.

Then he looks at the pager in my hand.

“Room 0 was removed from the directory,” he says.

“Two years ago.”

“Yes.”

“So it shouldn’t be able to send anything.”

“Correct.”

“Could it be a test?”

“Authorization would be required.”

“Could someone have accessed the old network?”

“Anyone with the right credentials could access almost anything. That’s why we audit.”

“Then who authorized this?”

He gives me the kind of look people use when they are deciding whether a child is frightened or merely disobedient.

“Get some rest when you can, Mara.”

“I’m working.”

“I know.”

He walks away.

The monitors keep time. Rain moves down the windows in silver threads. The ward smells of antiseptic, wet wool, and the burnt plastic from the old radiator beneath the nurses’ station.

A memory arrives without permission: my mother peeling an orange over the kitchen sink, the rind coming off in one long strip. She used to put the peel on the radiator so the whole house smelled sweet. The radiator clicked all winter. Click, pause, click. She said it was thinking.

I hear the same rhythm now from inside the wall.

Click.

Pause.

Click.

“Mara.”

Nia stands beside me.

“What?”

“You’re holding the pager too hard.”

I look down. The plastic has left a rectangle in my palm.

“I’m fine.”

My voice breaks on the first word.

Nia watches me for a moment. “That word is banned on this ward.”

“I’m checking something.”

“What?”

“The board.”

“There’s nothing on the board for Room 0.”

“Exactly.”

I leave the patients with Nia and walk to the central workstation. The electronic board is divided into columns: bed, patient, primary nurse, acuity, call status. I sign in. The system loads slowly, the blue bar crawling across the screen.

Mrs. Halloway: call light active.

Mr. Pritchard: medication overdue, then cleared.

Len Torres: telemetry disconnected.

The board has no entry for Room 0.

I open the paging log. The current shift appears in rows, each one stamped with time, sender, destination, and status. The log is usually dull enough to induce a coma.

02:01 — Room 12 — *Need bathroom assistance.*

02:08 — Room 4 — *Pain medication.*

02:14 — Room 7 — *Call light malfunction.*

02:26 — Room 0 — *Need water. Please check breathing.*

I stare at the line.

The sender field is blank.

I click it.

The system opens a detail panel.

`SOURCE: LIVE LOCATION`

Below that:

`LOCATION: 0`

I refresh the page.

The line remains.

I check the ward map. Room 1 is highlighted in blue. Room 2. Room 3. The map ends at the fire doors leading to the west service wing, which is shown as a gray block labeled *restricted access*.

There is no zero.

I open the location directory and search for 0.

One result appears.

`0 — LIVE`

The word pulses once beside the number.

Then the pager in my hand chirps.

This time the message is different.

`ROOM 0: MARA, PLEASE CHECK AGAIN.`

# Chapter 2: The Removed Room

“Can you check something?”

Nia hangs up the phone with her shoulder.

“If it’s your judgment, I already checked. It’s in short supply.”

“It’s not that.”

“Then it’s worse.”

I hand her the pager. Nia holds it by the edges, as if something could travel through the plastic and into her skin. The gray casing looks older in her hand. There is a crack near the side button and a peeling label on the back.

She reads the screen.

`ROOM 0: MARA, PLEASE CHECK AGAIN.`

“That’s English,” she says.

“Yes.”

“Who sends a page in English at two-thirty in the morning?”

“Someone using a system that shouldn’t be working.”

“Ah. So it’s the ghost of Information Technology.”

“Check it.”

“I am checking it.”

“Check it properly.”

Nia raises her eyes to me. The mascara beneath one eye is slightly smudged, though she never has time for makeup and I have never seen her arrive wearing any. Maybe she cried in the car. Maybe she rubbed her eye. She takes the pager back without arguing.

“I’ll check it properly.”

The central station sits between the two wings of Ward C, surrounded by medication cabinets, charging monitors, and a clean-linen cart someone has left across the corridor. The emergency lighting still glows in low strips along the baseboards. The ceiling lights are back, but they flicker now and then, as if the building is trying to remember.

Nia leads me to the small communications room behind the supervision desk. The door sticks in the frame. She hits it with her hip once, then again.

“This door has been like this since February,” she says.

“Since the renovation.”

“Since the promise of a renovation.”

Inside, an old terminal sits on a metal shelf. The monitor is square and thick, with a yellow stain in the lower corner. I remember it from when I started working at St. Brigid’s. Even then, it looked as though it had survived a shipwreck.

Nia presses the button.

Nothing happens.

She presses it again.

“You have to ask politely.”

“Please turn on.”

The monitor comes alive with a dry click. A blue screen appears, crowded with white lines. The cursor blinks in the corner. Nia tucks her hair behind her ear and opens a drawer.

“What do you keep in there?”

“Cables, adapters, a flashlight, two sugar packets, and a pizza receipt I never paid for.”

“Why?”

“Because the delivery guy was handsome.”

She finds a cable and connects it to the terminal. The screen changes. An old menu appears.

`CALL SYSTEM — MAINTENANCE MODE`

“Is this still connected to the network?”

“It shouldn’t be.”

“That sentence is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Nia looks at the screen, then at the pager.

“Do you want me to say it’s a glitch?”

“I want you to find out.”

“Is that different?”

“Yes.”

She types in the ward name. The keyboard sinks beneath her fingers with a typewriter clatter. The list of departments appears: emergency, surgical, ICU, obstetrics, east and north wings. West is marked inactive.

Nia clicks twice.

`WING WEST — REMOVED`

“Removed,” she says. “See? The computer knows.”

“It knows what someone told it to know.”

“It also knows you’re obsessed.”

“That’s not in the diagnosis.”

“I’m sure there’s a code for it.”

She opens the destination menu. The numbers begin at 1.

1. 2. 3.

The cursor stops, skips a line, and returns.

Nia frowns.

“It’s skipping twelve.”

“What do you mean?”

“It’s going from eleven to thirteen.”

“Room twelve is here.”

“I’m not saying it’s good at math.”

She scrolls to the end of the list. The number 0 does not appear. Then she goes back to the beginning and uses a combination of keys I don’t recognize.

“What was that?”

“Deleted-record filter.”

“You have access to that?”

“I have access to anything someone forgot to protect.”

“Is that supposed to reassure me?”

“No. You’ve worked here almost ten years.”

The screen flickers.

For a second, I see a line at the top of the monitor.

`DESTINATION: 0`

Then it disappears.

Nia goes still.

“What did you do?”

“Nothing.”

“You pressed something.”

“I pressed Enter. Enter doesn’t invent rooms.”

“Maybe it’s a nickname.”

“For what?”

“A temporary bed.”

“Temporary beds have bed numbers, not room numbers.”

“You asked if I had an explanation.”

“I asked if you had checked.”

She leans closer to the monitor. The blue reflection paints a second window across her face.

“It’s coming from a maintenance door,” she says.

“Which door?”

“I don’t know. The terminal records the source as a physical location, but it doesn’t show the map. Look.”

She points to a column.

`SOURCE: LIVE LOCATION`

Below it:

`NODE: 0`

“That’s impossible,” I say.

Nia doesn’t answer.

The air conditioner pushes a cold current through the floor grate. It smells of rust, disinfectant, and something salty. Seawater should not be getting into the building, but it comes into St. Brigid’s through every place that doesn’t appear in the reports.

“Is there an old map?” I ask.

“On paper?”

“Something.”

“Engineering archives are in the west.”

“I know.”

“It was rebuilt.”

“I know.”

“And the passage is blocked.”

“I know, Nia.”

She looks at me.

“You’re going there.”

It isn’t a question.

I take the flashlight from the drawer. My hands work. The left closes around the plastic body. The right holds the pager. The side button is wet. I don’t know whether it is water or sweat.

“I’m going to look at the wall.”

“Do you want me to come with you?”

“Yes.”

“I only asked because if you said no, I was going anyway.”

“Is that a good policy?”

“It’s a terrible policy. I’m still on probation.”

“Then stay in front of me.”

“Finally, some professional recognition.”

We leave the communications room. The station continues functioning around us: an infusion pump beeps in one of the rooms, someone asks for a blanket, the printer spits out a label no one requested. Patients breathe behind the doors. Some make noise. Some don’t.

Silence is worse.

Dishwashers. Why do people put forks point-up? Forks go down. It’s obvious. If someone falls with their hand inside, the person who loaded the machine will be responsible.

“Mara?”

“Nia?”

“You stopped.”

“I’m walking.”

“You stopped in the middle of the corridor.”

I start walking again.

The west fire door is past the service elevators. A red sign reads: `RESTRICTED ACCESS — CONSTRUCTION`. The plastic tape has been torn at shoulder height. The lock is not engaged.

Nia puts her hand on the handle.

“This wasn’t open.”

“How do you know?”

“I came through here twenty minutes ago. It was closed.”

“Someone from maintenance might have opened it.”

“At two forty in the morning?”

“Some people work nights.”

“You just told me maintenance doesn’t work nights.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said it with your face.”

The door opens onto a narrow corridor. The ceiling lights end three meters ahead. After that there is only the flashlight and the blue light coming through a high window at the end of the passage. Rain strikes the glass. The sound is muffled, like fingers against a box.

The west corridor was closed during reconstruction. I signed the transfer inventory: a few stretchers, several cabinets, a dressing cart, a wheelchair with a sticking right wheel. I remember the smell of wet plaster. I remember pencil marks on the walls showing where the cables would be cut.

I do not remember seeing a room.

The old plan showed Room 0 behind the service wing, against the outer wall. An improvised space for storm surges and overcrowding. The numbering began there, not in the main corridor. Room 0 came before Room 1, as if the building had taken one step backward before starting.

Nia walks ahead. Her sneaker slips on a thin layer of dust.

“Are you sure you want to do this without calling the supervisor?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because Elias will say it’s a technical fault.”

“And if it is?”

“Then he can say that after I confirm it.”

“You don’t trust him.”

“I trust him to say whatever is convenient.”

“That isn’t trust.”

“It’s the kind we have.”

She looks over her shoulder. The flashlight catches her face.

“You look tired.”

“I’m working.”

“That’s not what I said.”

“You’re working too.”

“I’m not receiving messages from a dead room.”

“It isn’t dead.”

“Rooms don’t die.”

“It was a figure of speech.”

“You’re very bad at calming me down.”

“I’m not trying to calm you down.”

“I know.”

At the end of the corridor is a smooth wall, freshly painted white. There is no door, frame, or sign. The paint is the same color as the new partitions in the east wing, but the surface beneath it is uneven, with a darker horizontal band at chest height.

I stop in front of the wall.

The dark line stretches from one side to the other. It is not dirt. The paint above it is swollen. Below, small blisters have opened and dried. The height matches the chest of a patient sitting in bed.

“That’s a flood mark,” I say.

Nia points the flashlight down.

Between the baseboard and the floor is a pale powder gathered in the corners. Demolition dust. Plaster, mortar, maybe cement. I press the tip of my glove into it. The powder sticks to the blue rubber.

“How long ago was the demolition?” Nia asks.

“Two years.”

“Then why is this still here?”

“Because nobody cleans behind a wall.”

“Is that what ghosts say?”

“They usually have other priorities.”

Nia runs her fingers over the paint. She stops before touching the dark band.

“Have you been here before?”

“During the transfer.”

“You said you signed the inventory.”

“I did.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“I came to the door.”

“Was the door here?”

I look at the empty space.

“It was.”

“Do you remember opening it?”

The question slips beneath my ribs. I see a corridor filled with water to the ankles. A stretcher against the wall. A sheet printed with blue and white ships, caught beneath the mattress. Someone’s hand striking a door three times.

Not here.

My mother used to put orange peels on the radiator when I was a child. The whole house smelled of burnt sugar. Jonah hated it. He said the smell got into his clothes. Once he threw the peels into the sink and the water carried everything down the drain, slowly, in circles.

“Mara.”

The wall returns.

“I remember.”

“What do you remember?”

“The door.”

“Was it open?”

“ No.”

“You just said you remember.”

“I remember it closed.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“I know.”

My pager vibrates in my hand. It is not a new message. Only the screen going dark.

`NODE: 0 — LIVE`

The corridor seems smaller. The ceiling lowers. A drop runs down the back of my neck. I wipe it away and find nothing.

“Let’s go back,” I say.

“You’re not going to knock on the wall?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it isn’t a door.”

“How do you know?”

“I’m looking at it.”

“You’re looking at a painted wall.”

“Exactly.”

Nia steps to the side. The flashlight beam crosses the section near the floor. There is an almost invisible mark on the baseboard: four parallel scratches, old, as if a stretcher scraped there. Something red is lodged in the paint at the center of the scratches. A piece of plastic, maybe.

She crouches.

“There’s something here.”

“Nia.”

“I’m just going to pick it up.”

“Nia.”

“It’s not an organ, Mara.”

She nudges the object with the tip of her pen. The plastic comes loose and drops to the floor. It is a transparent piece of label, with a blue stripe and half of a printed number.

`…0`

Nia does not touch it.

“You see that?”

“Yes.”

“Could that also be a nickname?”

“No.”

The pager clicks. We both raise our heads.

No message.

Something strikes the other side of the wall.

Once.

Nia stands too quickly and hits her shoulder against mine. The impact pushes me into the wall. Plaster vibrates beneath my back. We wait.

Nothing.

“Pipes,” she says.

“Maybe.”

“Thermal expansion.”

“Maybe.”

“A large rat.”

“It isn’t a rat.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You thought it.”

“I think many things. Some of them are useful.”

She moves closer to the wall but does not touch it. Her face changes when she hears the second sound: a long, dull pressure, like a hand sliding along the inside.

Then silence.

The patient in Room Six calls.

The voice travels through the open corridor toward the station.

“Nurse?”

Nia looks at me.

“Is that your phone?”

“No.”

“Then it’s a patient.”

“I heard it.”

“You’re going to answer.”

“Yes.”

The pager vibrates again.

Now there is a new line.

`ROOM 0: CHECK AGAIN.`

The call bell in Room Six rings at the same time, insistent. Once. Twice. Three times.

“Go,” I say.

Nia doesn’t move.

“Are you staying?”

“I’m going back to the station.”

“For the patient.”

“For the patient.”

She turns and starts walking. I follow. The corridor seems to stretch behind us. I don’t look at the wall. The urge to look is physical, almost a contraction.

Check again.

Room Six is lit. The door is open. Mr. Pritchard sits on the bed, leaning forward, one hand gripping the sheet and the other pressed to his chest.

Nia reaches him first.

“Mr. Pritchard, look at me.”

“I can’t—”

“You can talk. Breathe slowly.”

“My chest.”

I reach for the oximeter. His finger is cold. The reading takes time. 88. 86.

“Raise the head of the bed,” I say.

Nia presses the control. The bed rises with an electric groan.

“Blood pressure?”

“I’ll get it.”

“No. Pulse first.”

She touches his wrist. Counts silently, watching the station clock through the doorway.

“124. Irregular.”

“Call Dr. Bell. Bring the crash cart into the room.”

“I’m going.”

“Don’t go. Use the phone.”

She picks up the wall phone and dials.

“Dr. Bell? Nia in Six. Pritchard with chest pain, desaturation, irregular pulse. Yes. Now.”

I watch the skin around the patient’s mouth. It is gray. Sweat gathers along his scalp and runs down his temple. His breathing is short and shallow, without the effort I would expect. The body is trying to conserve itself.

“Mr. Pritchard, when did the pain start?”

“Now.”

“How long ago is now?”

“When I pressed it.”

“The button?”

He looks at the hand gripping the sheet.

“I called before.”

“I know.”

“He called before,” Nia confirms, still on the phone. “He says he called before.”

“How much before?”

“I don’t know. I pressed it and nobody came.”

The sentence hits me with a precision that has nothing to do with him. A flooded corridor. A closed door. Someone knocking. Three blows.

“How long ago?” I ask again.

“I don’t know.”

“Five minutes?”

“Maybe.”

“Ten?”

“I don’t know.”

Nia covers the mouthpiece.

“Do you want me to call the rapid-response team?”

“Yes.”

“And the cart?”

“Yes.”

She transmits the details. Her voice becomes strangely formal.

“Male patient, approximately seventy years old, acute chest pain, oxygen saturation eighty-six on room air, irregular pulse at one hundred twenty-four. Requesting immediate assessment.”

Pritchard tries to pull the sheet to his chest. His hand fails. I cover his knees with the fabric.

“I won’t leave you alone.”

“I pressed it.”

“I know.”

“It took too long.”

“I know.”

His eyes close.

The portable monitor arrives with a nurse I don’t know. She has a ladybug clip in her hair and brings the cart into the doorframe.

“Pritchard?” she asks.

“Yes. Put him on the monitor. Nia, get the suction. Mara, access.”

“He already has an IV in his left hand.”

“Then check it.”

The nurse looks at me, waiting for an order. The catheter is covered by a transparent dressing. The skin around it is not red. The fluid runs slowly.

“It’s patent,” I say.

“Did you check?”

“I’m checking.”

I disconnect the tubing and draw back. Blood returns. I flush with saline. The patient groans.

“Does it hurt?”

“No.”

“Then don’t move it.”

The nurse tapes the electrode to his chest. The machine prints an irregular line. Not a code, not normal, not mine to name before it has been assessed.

Dr. Bell enters, adjusting her ID badge in her pocket, without a coat. Her hair is flattened on one side.

“What have we got?”

“Chest pain, low saturation, irregular pulse. Started before the call was answered.”

“How much before?”

“He doesn’t know.”

“Blood pressure?”

The cuff inflates.

“One hundred ninety-two percent,” the nurse says.

“That would be an excellent blood pressure,” Nia replies.

The nurse looks at the paper.

“One hundred ninety-two percent?”

“One hundred ninety-two percent isn’t a blood pressure,” I say.

“One ninety-two over one-oh-eight,” she corrects.

Dr. Bell orders nitroglycerin, a twelve-lead ECG, and transfer to the monitored bay. The ward corridor is packed with extra beds. The monitored bay has one space, but the space is occupied by boxes of isolation supplies that should have been taken to storage.

“We’re moving the boxes,” I say.

“There’s no room for the monitor,” the nurse says.

“Then we make room.”

“Nurse—”

“Enfermeira,” I say.

“Enfermeira, protocol says we wait for transport.”

“He isn’t waiting.”

I pull the first box. The cardboard is damp and tears along the side. Solution bottles knock together. Nia grabs the second and pushes it with her foot.

“If anyone asks,” she says, “the ghost did it.”

“If anyone asks,” I reply, “you were providing care.”

“Also.”

The patient calls my name.

I don’t know how he knows my name.

“Mara.”

“I’m here.”

The monitor beeps faster. Dr. Bell bends over him.

“Mr. Pritchard, can you take a deep breath?”

“No.”

“Don’t force it.”

The pager sounds in my pocket.

One short vibration.

Then another.

I don’t look.

The cardiac monitor changes rhythm. The nurse calls for help. Nia clears a path through the corridor and shouts for another nurse. A door slams. Someone asks where the defibrillator is. Dr. Bell tells everyone to step back, then changes her mind and tells them to continue.

“Oxygen at four,” I say.

“He’s already on two.”

“I know.”

The mask covers Mr. Pritchard’s face. The elastic catches behind his neck. His chest rises, falls, rises.

The room narrows to hands, numbers, sounds.

IV patent.

High blood pressure.

Irregular pulse.

Saturation eighty-six.

I don’t want to think about the other room. I don’t want to think about the water. I don’t want to think about a patient who isn’t in this room and may never have existed.

The oximeter climbs to ninety-two.

“He’s responding,” the nurse says.

“Don’t celebrate,” Nia says. “He can hear you.”

“I’m not celebrating.”

“You made a face.”

“That’s my face.”

“I know. It’s an inconvenient face.”

The pager vibrates again.

I take it from my pocket.

The screen is empty.

No text. No source. Only the small envelope icon blinking.

Dr. Bell looks at me.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.”

The word comes out whole this time.

Nia watches me over the oxygen mask covering the patient.

“That word is banned too.”

“Focus on the patient.”

“I am focused on the patient. I can do two things.”

Pritchard opens his eyes.

“I thought you weren’t coming.”

Nia secures the monitor to the side of the bed.

“We came.”

“It took too long.”

No one answers.

The transport team finally appears. We take the bed to the monitored bay. The wheels catch on the floor beside the doors. I push from the head while Nia guides the cables. The corridor fills with people and objects: an IV stand, a trash bin, a chair shoved against the wall, somebody’s arm reaching through the gap.

In the empty room, the call bell keeps ringing.

When we return to the station, it stops.

The coffee is bad and I drink all of it.

Nia takes the cup from my hand before I can drop it.

“You’re shaking.”

“I’m not.”

She points at my hand.

The cup knocks against my teeth. Coffee spills over my wrist. I don’t feel the burn immediately. Then I do.

“Damn it.”

“Does that count as a clinical assessment?”

“It counts as coffee.”

She pulls a tissue from her pocket and wipes my skin. She does it automatically, looking toward the corridor instead of at me.

“Did you hear the wall?” I ask.

“I heard something.”

“Do you think it was a pipe?”

“No.”

“A rat?”

“It was a very polite rat. It knocked once, waited, then knocked again.”

“Are you saying it was a person?”

“I’m saying I don’t know.”

“That’s not what you usually say.”

“I usually say the system is twelve years old, the signs are wrong, and administration buys technology from paper catalogs.”

“But now?”

“Now I think someone is using a part of the hospital that was erased.”

“Someone alive?”

Nia rolls the wet tissue into her palm.

“Do you want an answer or an answer that lets you sleep?”

“An answer.”

“Then I don’t know.”

The station phone rings. Nia answers.

“Ward C.”

She listens.

“No, Room Six has been transferred. Yes. Just now.”

She looks at me.

“No, we don’t have a Room Zero.”

The person on the other end speaks for several seconds. The irony drains from Nia’s face.

“Who is calling?”

She listens again.

“I can’t hear you.”

The line drops. Nia sets the phone down slowly.

“Who was it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did you hear anything?”

“Noise. Water, maybe.”

“Water?”

“Or static.”

On the computer screen, the call from Room Six changes to `resolved`. The Room 0 line remains open.

`02:26 — 0 — Need water. Please check breathing.`

`SOURCE: LIVE LOCATION`

`LOCATION: 0`

Nia leans over the keyboard.

“I’m printing it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“If someone is deleting records—”

“Then we need paper.”

“Paper is still paper.”

“Not necessarily.”

“Are you listening to yourself?”

“Yes.”

“Are you scared?”

The question is simple. My throat closes before I can answer.

“I’m tired.”

“Mara.”

“I’m tired.”

The words come out rough. I look at the screen, at the open line, at the small column that says LIVE. The cursor blinks beside the number like a patient waiting for someone to decide.

Nia doesn’t press me. She opens the drawer and takes out her phone.

“I took a picture.”

“When?”

“Of the first page.”

“You took a picture?”

“I take pictures of everything.”

“Why?”

“Because when things disappear, everyone says I remembered wrong.”

“You can’t send that to anyone,” I say.

“I haven’t.”

“Not yet.”

“Not yet.”

“Elias already knows there was a page.”

“He doesn’t know about the wall.”

“He doesn’t know we went there.”

“How does that change—”

I don’t answer.

Nia puts the phone away.

“I’m keeping a copy.”

“Do it.”

“Do you want me to enter the message in the incident log?”

“No.”

She looks at me.

“You just said we need a record.”

“We need a record that can’t be corrected before the end of the shift.”

“You want to write it on paper?”

“I do.”

I take the incident log. The cover is greasy. I open it to a new page.

Date. Time. Location. Event.

My pen hovers over the line.

What do I write?

`Page received from a location that does not exist.`

`Wall with flood marks behind the west wing.`

`Patient in Room Six called while we investigated the destination.`

`The call was answered.`

The tip of the pen touches the paper. My hand starts shaking again. I grip my wrist with the other hand.

“Check again,” I say.

Nia looks up.

“Did you check?”

“I’m checking.”

I reread the screen. The first message remains. The second has disappeared. The old terminal is off in the room behind us. The west corridor is closed. Mr. Pritchard is being monitored. His saturation has reached ninety-five.

Everything is fine.

Everything is accounted for.

Everything is where it should be.

The pager vibrates.

It isn’t loud. It is the same small click as before, almost delicate. Even so, Nia slams the drawer shut.

I look at the device.

The message fills the screen.

`SHE IS NOT IN ROOM 0. SHE IS UNDER THE LIGHT.`
