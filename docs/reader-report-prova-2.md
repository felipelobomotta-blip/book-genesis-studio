# Reader's report — prova-2, chapters 1–3

Read straight through, blind, no outline, no briefs, no other files. Roughly 10,000 words in one sitting. What follows is what actually happened while reading, not what the text deserves on a rubric.

---

## 1. Where attention broke

Chapter 1 held me end to end. That is not a courtesy — Mr. Bell is genuinely well made. "He was seventy-eight, broad through the chest, and had a habit of apologizing before doing exactly what she told him not to do" is a sentence with a joke and a diagnosis in it, and the cannula argument that follows ("I can't breathe with it." / "You can't breathe without it." / "That doesn't make sense." / "It doesn't have to. It has to stay in your nose.") is the best writing in the three chapters. I did not want to stop.

The first break is chapter 2, the records office, from "Nora searched **413**" to "**RESTRICTED RECORD. CONTACT MEDICAL ADMINISTRATION.**" — about ninety lines. The database hands over exactly the right amount of information in exactly the right order, bolded, eight reveals with no friction: CLOSED, then the closure date, then the reason, then "Status: demolished," then the plan with the black rectangle, then "No further access required. Contents transferred or disposed of per emergency protocol," then the incident file number, then the lockout. Real archives are mostly the wrong file. This one is a quest-item dispenser. The only obstacle in the whole sequence is a gag — "My password expired." I stopped reading and started collecting.

The second break is worse because it is structural, not local: chapter 3, lines 477–501, respiratory arriving in East 217. "The room filled with people, equipment, clipped instructions. / 'Pressure?' someone asked. / 'Hundred and twelve,' Jonah said. / 'Sat eighty-nine.' / 'Keep him upright.' / 'Where's the physician?' / 'On the way.'" I skimmed it. Not because it is badly written — because I had already watched this exact rescue succeed in chapter 1 and I knew the number was going to climb back to the low nineties. It did. 91, then 93, then 94. In chapter 1 it was "Nora stayed at the bedside until the number reached 91."

So: nowhere in chapter 1. Twice after that, and the second time was fatal to my belief that the book was going somewhere.

---

## 2. Would I have turned to chapter 4

Yes — and only because of the last four lines. "**ROOM 413 / ALMA ORTIZ / RECEIVING BED:**" followed by an empty display and a blinking cursor is the single best horror beat in the manuscript, and it is the only one that isn't a jump scare. It is a form that cannot be completed. Alma Ortiz was never received anywhere. That is the book's actual idea, and it arrives in the final stroke of chapter 3, which means the idea is real and the book has been circling it for three chapters without saying it.

But I'd turn the page for the mystery, not for the prose, and I would be turning it suspiciously. If chapter 4 opens with a page and a patient at 86, I close the book. I'd give it one chapter of rope. I would not have gone to chapter 5 on the strength of chapters 1–3.

---

## 3. What I remember without scrolling back

Concretely, in the order they came back to me:

- The East Ward nurse "barefoot on one side. Her other shoe was caught in the wheel of a linen cart." Then the yellow ducks on the sock. Best physical observation in the book.
- "His breathing had turned shallow, almost polite." Best image in the book.
- "You have a catheter." / "I'm not using that." / "You don't have a choice." / "I have several choices." / "Not good ones."
- "I don't make promises to patients." / Miriam's mouth trembled. "You should."
- "There is now."
- The blinking cursor after the colon.
- Mr. Bell asking about his wife. "Is my wife here?" / "Not tonight." / "She said she'd come." He asks again in 408. Nobody ever tells us whether she comes. That is the most human thread in three chapters and it is dropped without comment.

What I do **not** remember is a person. I remember what Nora does; I could not tell you one thing she wants that is not "find out about 413." Jonah is a voice, not a man. Celia is a navy cardigan and a straightened badge. Daniel — the emotional engine, five separate flashbacks — comes back to me as "blood in a towel" and "a cold spoon," and I could not tell you whether he lived.

That is the finding: I retain about six images and one exchange out of ten thousand words, and the most vivid of them belongs to an unnamed nurse who exists for one scene. It is not only premise and atmosphere, but it is much closer to that than three chapters should be.

## 4. Does the voice hold, and what repeats

**The voice holds completely.** There is no drift at all: same clipped past tense, same one-sentence paragraphs, same refusal of adverbs, same untagged dialogue across all three chapters. That is real consistency, and read blind chapter by chapter it would look like control.

Read in sequence, holding and being stuck are indistinguishable. Counted repeats:

**The coffee sentence — 3×, verbatim, and twice it is impossible.** "The coffee was bad and she drank all of it." Chapter 1 after the empty catheter drawer, chapter 2 immediately after the tape sticks to her glove during a hypotensive emergency, chapter 3 immediately after she writes on the medication label. There is no coffee in the scene either of the last two times. Worse, the book has established that coffee is *Jonah's* prop — his cup goes cold in chapter 1, spills on the floor in chapter 1, and in chapter 2 "he lifted the cup, found it empty, and poured the remaining grounds into his mouth by mistake." Nora is never once shown obtaining coffee. Three identical sentences with no antecedent is not a motif; it is a refrain inserted by something that could not see the other two.

**Pager buzzes — 15×** (5 per chapter, dead even). Every single one runs the same three-beat: vibration, bolded all-caps line, characters looking at it. The book has exactly one lever for dread and pulls it fifteen times.

**The 03:17 / 03:16 clock disagreement — 6×.** Twice in chapter 1, twice in chapter 2, twice in chapter 3. The first time is excellent. The sixth time is furniture. The stopped second hand gag also fires twice: "The wall clock's red second hand moved forward, then stopped" (ch1) and "One. / Two. / Three. / It stopped." (ch2).

**Daniel flashbacks — 5×**, all with the same three-part machine: trigger word, kitchen image, then "She blinked." / "Nora blinked." / "She blinked." Three of the five exit on that exact sentence. And the content does not escalate: ch1 gives blood in a towel, ch2 gives blood on the table, ch3 retreats to a spoon under a faucet — *less* information in the third pass than the first.

**The wet pager — 5 beats**, all the same shape: "A bead of water had collected beneath the belt clip" (ch1), "Water had collected beneath the belt clip in a narrow dark line" (ch2), "A bead of water slid from its edge and dropped onto the desk" (ch2), "water had gathered again, a clear bead trembling with each step" (ch3).

**Celia's fatigue speech — 3×.** "Fatigue creates patterns" (ch1); the eleven-hours paragraph (ch2); "This is exactly how fatigue presents. You become certain that an unusual detail has personal significance" (ch3). She has one argument and delivers it three times in one night. Her badge is described 3×, the navy cardigan 4×. Her non-expression is described eight ways — "expression did not move," "face remained calm," "expression stayed soft," "gaze remained gentle," "voice stayed gentle," "voice remained patient," "voice became almost kind," "face did not change, but the warmth left her eyes." Her only registered emotion is the absence of registered emotion.

**Jonah's jokes — roughly 15, all one template**: literal-minded pedantry aimed at institutional language. "That is different from having four beds." "I understand what the word available means." "Because it's empty. That's generally how emptiness works." "You can't put a stretcher on top of another stretcher and call it surge capacity." "That's why they're medically indicated." "I lied to create momentum." "Because you have the survival instincts of a moth." "Maybe the ghost has excellent charting habits." He is never frightened without a joke attached, never quiet, never wrong. By chapter 3 he is a laugh track. The text half-knows — "He rubbed his mouth. 'That one was for me.'" — but knowing is not varying.

**"That isn't an answer" — 8×**, and this is the one that actually breaks the voice work. "That wasn't what I asked" (ch1, operator). "That isn't an answer" (ch2, Celia). "That wasn't my question" (ch2, Jonah). "That isn't what I asked" (ch2, Nora). "That's not an answer" (ch3, Nora). "That isn't what I asked" (ch3, **Miriam**). "You asked whether she left him unattended" (ch3, Nora). "That wasn't my question" (ch3, Nora). When Miriam Cole, dying at 86 systolic, reaches for the same conversational move as Nora, Jonah and Celia, the differentiation has collapsed. Everyone in this hospital argues in the same style, and that style is "correct the premise of the last line."

**The triplet-of-absence sentence — 3×**: "The display still carried neither timestamp nor sender code." / "The record carried no patient name, bed designation, or transfer destination." / "The display had none of the usual fields: timestamp, sender code, callback." That is one template refilled three times.

## 5. Continuity and arc

This is where the sequence reading pays, and the news is bad.

**Chapters 1 and 3 are the same chapter.** Beat for beat: page arrives → authority disbelieves → an elderly man desaturates → wall suction fails → portable suction battery is dead → Nora clears the airway by hand → the number staircases back to the low nineties → Celia arrives and reframes it as fatigue → a new page ends the chapter. Even the failure order is identical: "The suction's dead" then "Its battery indicator flashed red" (ch1); "Wall suction is at zero" then "The battery's dead" (ch3). Bell falls 94→69 and climbs to 91; Laird falls 86→74 and climbs to 94, both rendered as isolated numbers on their own lines. That is not escalation. That is a reprise with the props renamed, and no chapter-level judge could ever see it.

**Chapter 3 does not know what chapter 2 established.** Chapter 2's best discovery is handwriting on a scanned plan: "**temporary hold during evacuation**," with "**ISO.**" crossed out beneath the room number. In chapter 3, Celia's big extracted confession — staged with "Celia took a breath through her nose," a full page of resistance, "That wasn't my question," "Yes." — is: "Room 413 was a temporary holding room during the evacuation." The reader already owns that fact, from a document, in chapter 2. The novel dramatises the reluctant surrender of information it already gave away for free. That is an arc failure that only exists between chapters.

**The clock does not move for an hour.** Chapter 1 runs 03:12 → 03:16. Chapter 2 then contains: Celia confiscating the pager, a full assessment of Miriam, a new line, an intranet floor plan, a document-history search, a facilities call about a flooding ambulance ramp, an explicit order to "stay until the replacement has been running fifteen minutes," Nora complying ("stayed beside her until the replacement had run long enough"), a full BP cycle, a walk to the records office behind admissions, four database searches, and a lockout — and the wall clock outside the records office reads **03:16**. Chapter 3 opens at **03:16**, runs an elevator ride, a stuck elevator, and a complete resuscitation, and the clock above the sink in East 217 reads **03:16**. Roughly an hour of action across four minutes of wall time. The text does flirt with a frozen clock, but no character ever notices — and Nora is a character who pockets a medication label as evidence and writes "03:16 wall clock" on it without remarking that it said 03:16 an hour ago. Either the timeline is broken or the freeze is the most alarming fact in the hospital and the most observant person in it is blind to it.

**The eleven hours are wrong, and chapter 2 copies the error.** Ch1: "How long have you been on shift?" / "Since nineteen hundred." / "That's eleven hours." / "It's three in the morning." 19:00 to 03:00 is eight hours. Celia is out by three. Chapter 2 then repeats it — "A nurse working eleven hours into a night shift." This is load-bearing: the fatigue argument is the antagonist's entire case, and the difference between eight hours and eleven is the difference between a shift and a hazard. The book's central counter-argument rests on arithmetic nobody checked, and the error propagated forward rather than being caught.

**The elevator goes the wrong way.** West ward is the 400s. East 217 is the second floor. Jonah tells facilities they're stuck "between five and six," and "the elevator began to climb" before it "jolted downward" and opened on East Ward. Four, up to between five and six, then down to two, with no explanation for the climb. In a novel whose horror *is* a room number, being sloppy with floors and numbers is not a small sin — it teaches the reader not to trust the geography that the scare depends on.

**Nora's handoff is voided two paragraphs after she gives it.** Chapter 3's best character beat is her handing Miriam's chart to Jonah with precise instructions, including the lovely ch1 callback "His suction is plugged into the red outlet, not the wall beside the bed." Forty lines later Jonah is in the elevator with her: "Lena's covering her." The book wanted the professionalism speech and also wanted Jonah for banter, and did not notice the second cancels the first.

**Miriam is the dropped card.** She is the only independent corroboration in the book: "They said the other room" (ch1), "The water," then in ch2 she says the number out loud — "Four thirteen" — and "Not current," and "It's still running," and "You have to tell them." Chapter 3 reduces her to a number Jonah shouts from a doorway. Nobody ever asks her what she meant. In sequence that reads as the book forgetting its strongest move within one chapter of making it.

**Jonah's leak contradicts itself.** Ch2: "My old apartment had a leak under the kitchen sink" — past tense, old apartment, a landlord. Ch3: a plumber calls, "He says the kitchen sink is leaking again" — present, current apartment. Either "old" is wrong, or both his apartments had kitchen-sink leaks and nobody makes the joke.

**Two threads pointed at and never picked up.** ISO on the 413 plan (ch2) and the locked isolation room 215 that keeps the oxygen cylinder out of reach (ch3) — nobody in the text so much as glances at the echo. And the stuck elevator, with its "soft metallic knock above the ceiling" and swaying car, costs nothing, means nothing, and is never mentioned again. That is dread as decoration, in the chapter that could least afford filler.

## 6. Person or machine — the exact sentences

**Written by a person, clearly:**

Everything clinical. "Nora tightened the strap around his wrist, checked the IV site, then looked at the monitor again." "The line had infiltrated. A cool swelling spread beneath the dressing." "The vein rolled. She withdrew, tried again, and got a flash." "Air on both sides, reduced at the bases." "Her handwriting had become narrow and hard." "not numbers but drops, each one a small clear body falling through the tube." "Her legs felt loose below the knees." Whoever wrote the nursing knew the nursing, and the specificity is where all the book's authority comes from.

**Generated, and I can point at it:**

- "The coffee was bad and she drank all of it," three times, twice with no coffee in the scene. Nothing else in the manuscript indicts it as hard as this does.
- "She wondered whether the cafeteria had finally fixed the coffee machine that dispensed hot water under the label DECAF." Inserted into an elevator while she is running to a patient at 86%. That is a quirk fired on a schedule, not a thought a person has in that elevator.
- The banana. "He peeled the rest of the banana and took one bite. / 'You hate bananas,' she said. / 'I know.'" — then it reappears twice as scenery ("Jonah looked down at the abandoned banana," "Jonah's banana turning brown at the edge") purely to demonstrate that it was planted. Quirk-as-characterisation, then quirk-as-callback.
- "She looked down at it as if she had only just discovered it" — and then the text names her "the duck-sock nurse," three times. The shoe in the linen cart is a real observation; labelling her by it afterwards is the text congratulating itself.
- The explain-the-last-line abstractions, one per chapter: "The answer sat in the room with them, but it was not useful yet." / "The honesty of it sat between them." / "The warning had become a number moving down beneath her fingers." Each sounds good alone. Three of them is a habit — the prose keeps telling you what the previous beat meant instead of trusting it.
- "The words were ordinary. They were also the first thing Daniel had said after the blood appeared through the towel." Keyword-triggered memory: Miriam says "Stay with me," and the flashback engine fires on the phrase. Chapter 1 does the same with "The water." Chapter 3's fires on nothing at all.
- "Eight-oh-nine needs a new peripheral line," he said. "Forty-six wants morphine" — on a ward numbered 401 to 414. Filler that sounds like a hospital rather than being one.

The pattern is coherent and worth naming: this text knows how a ward works and does not know how dread works. The procedural writing is earned; the horror writing is a template with slots.

---

## 7. Would I pay for this book

No — not at novel price on this evidence. I'd read it free, and I'd have paid for it if the sample had stopped at the end of chapter 2.

---

## The short version

Chapter 1 is good enough that I would have recommended it. Chapter 2 is the most interesting chapter and the only one that adds anything. Chapter 3 is chapter 1 again with a different old man, and it hands back information chapter 2 already gave me while a clock that has not moved in an hour sits on the wall behind everyone.

Three chapters approved one at a time will always miss exactly this, because none of it is visible inside a chapter. The repetitions are invisible at n=1 (one coffee sentence is a nice line; three is a machine). The reprise is invisible at n=1 (chapter 3 is a well-built rescue; it is the *second* well-built rescue). The eleven hours is invisible at n=1 (it is only wrong if you check it, and chapter 2 copied it instead of checking). The frozen clock is invisible at n=1 by definition.

The idea underneath — a holding room used during an evacuation, patients "transferred or disposed of per emergency protocol" with no destination recorded, a pager one minute ahead of a hospital that has already decided not to write things down — is a genuinely good idea, and the blinking cursor after "RECEIVING BED:" proves the book knows it. It has not started telling that story yet. It has told the same shift three times while waiting.
